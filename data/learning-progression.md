# HVAC Learning Progression Research
**For a personalized HVAC trainee app (Match My Path)**

- Research date: 10 November 2026 (America/Chicago)
- Purpose: Evidence-based learning sequence, first steps, personalization rules, certification timing, and early on-the-job tasks for five paths: Residential Service Tech, Residential Installer, Commercial Service Tech, Commercial Installer, and a Sales track for service techs and installers.
- Method: Public curricula and standards from apprenticeship programs, entry-level competency exams, EPA regulations, trainer content, and contractor/association guidance. Facts are tied to cited URLs. Where a calendar (week 1 / 30 / 60 / 90 / months 3–12) is recommended, it is a **synthesis for onboarding and in-app sequencing**, not a published national standard.

## Important caveats (read first)

1. **A 30/60/90 plan is not an apprenticeship.** Registered HVAC apprenticeships commonly run about **four to five years** with on-the-job learning on the order of **8,000 hours**, plus related classroom instruction (NCCER four-level HVACR curriculum is written to align with U.S. Department of Labor apprenticeship standards; a USDOL work-process example totals 8,000 OJT hours). The calendar below is a practical **first-year onboarding and study sequence** for helpers and new apprentices. Advancement should be gated by **demonstrated competency and supervision**, not by the calendar alone.
2. **State and local licensing varies** and can legally restrict independent work, gas piping, electrical connections, or even the title “technician.” This document does not replace local license research. Uncertainty: requirements were not inventoried state-by-state.
3. **Climate, employer, and equipment mix change order.** A northern residential contractor may put gas or oil heat ahead of cooling; a cooling-dominated market does the reverse. Commercial shops that run chillers will introduce Type III topics earlier than a rooftop-only shop.
4. **IEC HVAC curriculum.** IEC is a major electrical apprenticeship organization; at least one chapter has announced HVAC apprenticeship expansion, but a national, public IEC HVAC module outline comparable to NCCER or UA was **not found**. ABC chapters commonly use **NCCER**.
5. **NATE experience guidance is not identical across documents.** The NATE Technician Handbook suggests “Core, Installation, or Service Certification Exams: 2 years.” The NATE FAQ (page updated 26 July 2023) recommends **one year** for installation technician tests and **two years** for service technician tests. Both are recommendations, not eligibility rules (anyone may sit for the exams).
6. **Do not treat this app’s internal certificates as EPA, NATE, OSHA, or state licenses.**

## (b) What absolute beginners should start with vs. experienced learners

### Absolute beginners — start here (before path-specific diagnostics)

Apprenticeship year-one outlines, NCCER Core + HVACR Level 1, the ESCO Institute one-year program guideline, and NATE Ready-to-Work Knowledge Areas of Technician Expertise (KATEs) converge on the same foundation. HVAC School’s “Start Here” path adds a practical *conceptual* order once safety and tools are in place.

**Week 1 priorities (all paths):**

| Order | Topic | Why it comes first | Primary sources |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Worksite and personal safety: PPE, ladders, driving, refrigerant awareness, general job-site hazards | Prevents injury; NATE Ready-to-Work weights general safety at 14% of the entry exam; UA and ABC put OSHA and tools/safety in year one | NATE Ready-to-Work KATE PDF; UA Local 464 first-year objectives; ABC Virginia HVAC I |
| 2 | Electrical safety (including lockout/tagout awareness and not working energized without training and authorization) | Electrical work is a leading HVAC hazard; Ready-to-Work weights electrical safety at 12%; OSHA 10 is awareness training, not a substitute for employer hazard-specific training | NATE RTW KATE; OSHA Outreach FAQs; ESCO one-year Safety course |
| 3 | Tool identification, selection, care, and safe use | Ready-to-Work weights tools at 32% — the largest domain; UA first-year objectives explicitly include identification and care of tools | NATE RTW KATE; UA LU 464 curriculum PDF |
| 4 | Measurements and units (rulers, area, volume, temperature, pressure awareness) | Needed before charging, airflow, or layout work; Ready-to-Work 8%; NCCER Trade Mathematics is a Level 1 module | NATE RTW KATE; NCCER HVACR Level 1 |
| 5 | Professional habits and shadowing: show up prepared, stay hydrated, wear eye protection, ask questions, do not guess on live equipment, final walk-around before leaving | Field advice for new techs, not a formal standard | HVAC School, Bryan Orr, “Tips for Newbies” and “4 Silly Mistakes of The New HVAC Tech” |
| 6 | Component identification (what the major parts are, not how to diagnose them) | Ready-to-Work weights components at 22% | NATE RTW KATE |

**First 30 days conceptual core (study + supervised observation):**

1. **Heat transfer** — conduction, convection, radiation; sensible vs. latent heat (NATE RTW 12% basic heat transfer; HVAC School tips 4–6; ESCO “Heat, Temperature and Pressure”; NCCER Laws of Thermodynamics in 03101).
2. **The vapor-compression refrigeration cycle and four major components** — compressor, condenser, metering device, evaporator; saturation and the pressure-temperature relationship (HVAC School starts its “Start Here” path here; NCCER 03107 Introduction to Cooling; ESCO HVACR Fundamentals).
3. **Basic electricity for safety and usefulness** — voltage, current, resistance, Ohm’s law, meter basics (ohms/continuity), 240-volt awareness. New techs should **measure only as trained and authorized**, not troubleshoot live equipment alone (NCCER 03106; ESCO Electricity is the largest block in the one-year outline at ~140 hours; HVAC School electrical basics tips).
4. **Introduction to heating and air distribution at a recognition level** — furnace types and combustion awareness; filters, supply/return, why dirty filters and blocked drains matter (NCCER 03108, 03109).
5. **EPA Section 608 awareness** — what activities legally require certification, and that unsupervised gauge/hose or charge/recovery work is not a beginner task.

HVAC School’s published “Start Here” order for *concepts* is: refrigeration-cycle physics → measurements (superheat, subcooling, delta T, charging) → airflow/static pressure → electrical basics → humidity/heat pumps. That order is excellent for study. It should sit **on top of** week-1 safety, tools, and supervised habits from apprenticeship curricula. A new helper who jumps to charging methods before electrical safety, tool competence, and EPA rules is out of sequence with both law and apprenticeship practice.

**First on-the-job tasks for beginners** (all paths, under supervision): material handling and housekeeping; filter changes; condensate pan/drain inspection and clearing; assisting with coil cleaning; carrying and staging tools; reading the work order; greeting the customer or site contact; documenting what was observed; a final walk (disconnects, caps, setpoints, screws, cleanup). These match NATE Ready-to-Work domains (components, tools, safety) and common year-one helper work. They do **not** include unsupervised refrigerant recovery, charging, live electrical diagnosis, gas-valve work, or independent troubleshooting.

### If the person already has experience or schooling

Do **not** restart at week 1 if they can demonstrate competence. Assess, then place.

| Profile | Recommended start | Skip or test-out | Still required or strongly advised |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Trade-school graduate, little field time | Short safety/jobsite orientation + tool/shop standards of *this* employer; then supervised OJT in their path; fill gaps the school did not cover (often airflow measurement, customer communication, or documentation) | Foundational classroom modules they already passed (confirm with transcript or a placement quiz) | EPA 608 if not already held before independent refrigerant-circuit work; employer-specific safety; NATE Ready-to-Work or HVAC Excellence Employment Ready exams as validation if not taken |
| Helper with 0–6 months, no school | Keep foundation modules; accelerate only topics they already do daily (e.g., filters, drains); add cycle and electrical theory in parallel with work | Pure orientation content they have already lived | EPA 608 timing; OSHA 10 if not completed; Ready-to-Work when ready |
| 6–12 months in the field | NATE HVAC Support Technician window (NATE: designed for techs with 6–12 months); path-specific service or install modules; start consistent call/install process and options talking | Ready-to-Work-level tool/component ID if already mastered | EPA 608 if still missing; deepening electrical and airflow |
| 1–2+ years, changing paths (e.g., residential service → commercial service) | Path-delta modules only (rooftop safety, three-phase, belts, economizers, BAS trend reading, hydronics, etc.) plus a short safety delta | Shared refrigeration-cycle and basic electrical modules if competence is demonstrated | Certifications matching new equipment (Type III before low-pressure chiller work; Universal if the shop needs all types) |
| Already EPA 608 certified | Do not repeat 608 content except as a refresher or for new refrigerant classes (e.g., A2L/low-GWP safety if not trained) | 608 exam prep | Still complete safety, tools, and path skills |
| Already OSHA 10 or 30 | Do not repeat the same Outreach card | OSHA Outreach course | Employer-specific hazards still required; OSHA states Outreach training does **not** satisfy all OSHA standard-required training |
| NATE professional certification already held | Place into advanced path modules and recertification/CE tracking | Entry certificates and early fundamentals if KATEs are already demonstrated | Recertification cycle (professional NATE certifications: every two years, per NATE FAQ) |
| Experienced electrician entering HVAC | Safety delta for refrigerants, combustion, brazing, and EPA 608; keep electrical modules at a higher starting point (diagrams, HVAC loads, motors) | Basic Ohm’s law / meter use if already competent | EPA 608; heat-transfer and refrigeration-cycle modules; combustion safety if doing gas |

## (a) Recommended learning sequence by path

Stages used below:

- **Week 1**
- **First 30 days** (includes week 1, then builds)
- **Days 31–60**
- **Days 61–90**
- **Months 3–12** (often split 3–6 and 6–12)

Shared year-one classroom backbone (then specialize):

**NCCER HVACR Level 1 (6th ed., 2022)** after Core: Introduction to HVACR (03101, 5 hrs); Trade Mathematics (03102, 10 hrs); Basic Electricity (03106, 15 hrs); Introduction to Heating (03108, 15 hrs); Introduction to Cooling (03107, 30 hrs); Air Distribution Systems (03109, 15 hrs); Basic Copper and Plastic Piping Practices (03103, 12.5 hrs); Soldering and Brazing (03104, 10 hrs); Basic Carbon Steel Piping Practices (03105, 10 hrs). Level 1 is listed as 200 hours including Core.

**NCCER Core** typically includes basic safety, construction math, hand tools, power tools, construction drawings, and related employability / materials-handling skills (used by ABC as the year-one front end).

**UA year-one examples** emphasize tools and safety, math, OSHA-30 (common in UA programs, vs. OSHA 10 in many ABC programs), first aid/CPR, copper soldering/brazing, basic electricity, and basic refrigeration & A/C, plus shop time (UA-NJ; UA Local 100; UA Local 464).

**ESCO Institute one-year guideline** (hours are approximate): Industry overview (5); Safety (20); Heat, temperature and pressure (20); HVACR fundamentals (50); System configurations (15); Heat pumps (20); Refrigerants and EPA 608 (30); Electricity (140); Airflow (30); Piping (30); Installation, start-up, and maintenance (50); Troubleshooting (40). Total ~450 hours. This is a full-time school-year model, denser than a working apprentice’s classroom night schedule.

**ABC Virginia / similar ABC chapters** place Core, Intro to HVAC, math, electricity, intro heating/cooling/air distribution, copper/plastic piping, soldering and brazing, carbon steel piping, First Aid/CPR, and often OSHA 10 and EPA 608 in year one; year two adds leak detection/evacuation/recovery/charging, AC theory, heat pumps, duct systems, troubleshooting, hydronics, etc.

### 1. Residential Service Tech

**Week 1.** Company and job-site safety orientation; PPE, ladder, and vehicle safety; tool inventory and care; shadow a senior tech on maintenance or simple service calls; learn the work-order and parts process; customer greeting and professional appearance; observe only on electrical and refrigerant work; practice a final walk-around (caps, disconnects, thermostat setpoints, cleanup) as described by HVAC School.

**First 30 days.** Heat transfer and refrigeration-cycle fundamentals (study). Electrical safety and meter basics (supervised). Component ID on residential split systems, furnaces, and air handlers. Daily helper tasks: filters, condensate drains/pans, outdoor-coil cleaning assist, blower-compartment awareness (do not reach into moving parts), reporting filter and drain condition to the customer. Start OSHA 10 Construction if the employer or program requires it (ABC-style) or follow the program’s OSHA-30 path (UA-style). Begin NATE Ready-to-Work study (components, tools, measurements, electrical safety, heat transfer, general safety).

**Days 31–60.** Deeper cycle: saturation, P-T relationship, what gauges mean (still no unsupervised gauge work). Introduction to superheat, subcooling, and delta T as *ideas*, with a senior tech taking the readings. Basic wiring-diagram symbols. Gas-furnace or heat-pump recognition depending on climate and inventory. EPA 608 Core + Type II prep (Type I optional; Type III usually not needed for pure residential comfort cooling). Copper tubing practices and brazing lab or supervised field joints. Continue maintenance PMs with increasing independence on non-refrigerant tasks.

**Days 61–90.** After EPA 608 (or while remaining closely and continually supervised as an apprentice under 40 CFR 82.161), assist with recovery, leak check, evacuation, and charging. Airflow fundamentals: why a dirty filter or restricted return causes freeze-ups (HVAC School). Simple, supervised electrical checks (e.g., verifying a disconnected disconnect is open; continuity on a removed component). Run a consistent maintenance call start-to-finish under observation. Sit for NATE Ready-to-Work when ready (NATE: entry-level to ~6 months). Introduce ACCA-style communication: explain findings in plain language; do not sell beyond competence.

**Months 3–6.** Preventive maintenance as a repeatable process (NCCER 03215 Basic Maintenance is a Level 2 module: inspection, belts, gaskets, selected equipment). Heat pumps (defrost, reversing valve, auxiliary heat) and/or gas furnaces (combustion safety, ignition sequence) to match equipment exposure. Air distribution and static-pressure awareness. Electrical: capacitors, contactors, transformers, basic schematic reading. Supervised diagnostics on common no-cool / no-heat calls. HVAC Excellence Employment Ready Electrical and/or Air Conditioning or Heat Pumps if in a school or assessment setting. Start maintenance-agreement conversations only after the tech can complete a thorough maintenance and explain findings (see Sales track).

**Months 6–12.** Troubleshooting foundations: airflow vs. charge vs. electrical, using measurements rather than parts-changing (HVAC School measurement path; ESCO troubleshooting course objectives; NCCER later troubleshooting modules belong here as *introduction*, not mastery). Ductless/mini-split basics if the company installs or services them (NCCER 03315 is a later module; ESCO and HVAC Excellence offer Mini-Split Core / Employment Ready). IAQ accessories (filters, UV, humidifiers) at a service level. NATE HVAC Support Technician certificate is designed for **6–12 months** in the field (proctored; certificate, not professional NATE certification). Professional NATE Core + a residential Service specialty (Air Conditioning, Air-to-Air Heat Pump, or Gas Heating) is generally **too early** unless the person already has substantial experience; NATE’s more recent FAQ suggests **two years** before service exams. Months 6–12 should build the experience those exams assume.

**Next steps after month 12 (context, not this year’s core):** continued supervised diagnostics, manufacturer training, then NATE service certification when experience supports it; optional hydronics or refrigeration if goals change.

### 2. Residential Installer

**Week 1.** Same safety, tools, and shadowing as service, plus **jobsite and new-construction / replacement etiquette** (protect floors, cover furniture, material staging, lifting with a team). Introduction to construction drawings at a recognition level (NCCER Core includes introduction to construction drawings). Housekeeping and punch-list awareness.

**First 30 days.** Shared fundamentals: heat transfer, cycle overview (so they understand *why* line-set practice, evacuation, and airflow matter), electrical safety, trade math (measurement, level, layout). Helper install tasks: staging equipment and materials, assisting with pads and setting (not solo rigging), running and securing flex or helping with metal duct, insulating line sets, condensate piping assist, sealing wall penetrations, cleanup. OSHA 10 (or 30 per program). Ready-to-Work study.

**Days 31–60.** Copper and plastic piping practices; soldering and brazing skill-building (NCCER 03103–03104; UA year-one brazing; ESCO piping + Employment Ready Brazing and Soldering). Introduction to heating and cooling equipment placement rules at a basic level. Low- and line-voltage wiring assist under supervision (whips, thermostat cable, disconnects — no unsupervised terminations). Duct basics: flex connections, sealing, support, return drop awareness. EPA 608 Core + Type II prep (required before they independently open a refrigerant circuit). Combustion/venting awareness if installing furnaces (NCCER 03202 Chimneys, Vents and Flues is typically Level 2; introduce hazards early, full competence later).

**Days 61–90.** Assist complete changeouts start-to-finish: recover (certified or closely supervised apprentice), remove, set, connect, nitrogen pressure test / leak test, evacuate, charge, and start-up **with a lead installer**. Airflow verification *before* final charging adjustments — this is a core ACCA Quality Installation practice (verify airflow/water flow, then charge; document). Thermostat setup under supervision. Final walk and customer orientation (how to use the system, filter sizes, warranty paperwork). Ready-to-Work exam when ready.

**Months 3–6.** Repeatable quality-install sequence aligned with ACCA QI field practices: airflow, duct leakage awareness, electrical measurements, refrigerant charge within OEM targets, combustion where applicable, and **documentation**. Heat-pump installation specifics (lines, electrical, low-voltage, auxiliary heat). Manuals and OEM installation instructions as a habit (HVAC School: read product manuals). Mini-split / ductless install fundamentals if the company offers them. Employment Ready Brazing and Soldering; Employment Ready System Recovery and Evacuation if in a school setting. Sales-adjacent skills: explaining install options and maintenance plans at turnover (see Sales track).

**Months 6–12.** Independently execute more of the install under a lead, then progress toward lead-helper or second-chair on simple changeouts as competence is signed off. Air distribution fitting and sealing quality; static pressure and CFM checks. Gas piping only if licensed/authorized and trained. Load-calculation and duct-design awareness (NCCER Level 4 territory for full design — months 6–12 is *awareness and measuring existing systems*, not independent Manual J/D/S practice unless separately trained). NATE HVAC Support Technician at 6–12 months. NATE **Installation** specialty (Air Conditioning, Air Distribution, Air-to-Air Heat Pump, or Gas Heating) is recommended around **one year** per the NATE FAQ (handbook groups it with a two-year suggestion — note the discrepancy). ACCA Quality Installation training is a strong installer credential path when the contractor uses QI.

### 3. Commercial Service Tech

**Week 1.** All shared safety, plus **commercial site rules**: roof access, fall protection awareness, lockout/tagout, confined-space awareness if relevant, customer/site check-in, badging, and never bypassing building procedures. Tool and PPE expectations for rooftop and mechanical-room work. Shadow PMs on rooftop units (RTUs) or indoor equipment. Housekeeping and documentation of readings on the company’s PM form.

**First 30 days.** Shared heat-transfer, cycle, and electrical-safety fundamentals. Commercial component ID: RTU sections, filters, belts, pulleys, economizers (recognition only), disconnects, condensate. Helper PM tasks: filters, belt *inspection* (adjustment later under supervision), coil cleaning assist, lubricating only as trained, recording nameplate and hour-meter/runtime data. OSHA 10 or 30. Ready-to-Work study still applies (entry-level knowledge is the same). Three-phase *awareness* can start, but not independent three-phase troubleshooting.

**Days 31–60.** Basic electricity including introduction to three-phase and larger motors (NCCER 03206 Alternating Current is Level 2). Refrigeration cycle on packaged commercial equipment. EPA 608 Core + Type II (covers high- and very high-pressure appliances used in most rooftop and split commercial comfort systems). Type III only if the shop services **low-pressure** appliances (centrifugal chillers, etc.). Piping/brazing as needed. Read PM checklists and begin taking supervised temperature, amp, and pressure observations.

**Days 61–90.** Supervised leak check, recovery, and charging on accessible systems after certification or under apprentice supervision rules. Introduction to commercial airside concepts (constant volume vs. VAV recognition — NCCER 03201 is a later module; keep this at awareness). Belt replacement and pulley alignment under supervision (USDOL work process includes “align pulleys, bearing blocks, belt tension” under system maintenance). Customer/site communication: clear, documented findings to a facility manager (different from residential homeowners). Ready-to-Work exam when ready.

**Months 3–6.** Repeatable commercial PM and basic service: airflow and economizer awareness, filters/belts/coils, electrical measurements, refrigeration measurements. Introduction to building automation: **read trends, alarms, and schedules; do not program controllers** at this stage (industry career guidance consistently places BAS programming later). Light commercial refrigeration only if that is the shop’s work (NCCER retail refrigeration is a later module). Heat pumps or gas heat as installed on RTUs. Employment Ready Light Commercial Air Conditioning if in a school/assessment setting.

**Months 6–12.** Troubleshooting packaged rooftop and split commercial systems with a lead. Motor and control-circuit fundamentals (NCCER 03317, 03318 are Level 3-style skills — introduce, do not claim journey-level competence). If the path includes chillers: cooling-tower and pump awareness, logs, water-treatment *awareness* (not chemistry competence), and Type III or Universal **before** independent low-pressure refrigerant work. Hydronic basics if the account mix includes boilers/chilled water (NCCER hydronic modules). NATE Support Technician at 6–12 months. Professional NATE service specialties such as Light Commercial Refrigeration or Commercial Refrigeration, or Air Conditioning Service, belong after more experience (NATE suggests two years for service tests). Uncertainty: there is no single “commercial RTU” NATE specialty; contractors typically map to Air Conditioning, Heat Pump, or refrigeration specialties plus manufacturer training.

### 4. Commercial Installer

**Week 1.** Shared safety plus construction-site safety, rigging *awareness* (NCCER Core includes a rigging overview; USDOL equipment-installation work process includes slings, hoists, rollers, dollies), crane day etiquette (stay clear, follow the signal person), and drawing/spec recognition. Material handling and housekeeping on occupied commercial sites.

**First 30 days.** Trade math, electrical safety, cycle overview, and tool competence. Helper tasks: staging, curb and unit-setting assist (never exceed trained rigging role), duct connections assist, hanging and supporting pipe/duct, condensate, insulation, penetration sealing, fire-stop awareness (flag uncertainty: fire-stopping may require a specific trade or certification on some sites). OSHA 10 or 30. Ready-to-Work study.

**Days 31–60.** Piping: copper, plastic, and carbon steel practices (NCCER 03103–03105) — commercial work uses steel and larger pipe more than typical residential changeouts. Soldering and brazing. Electrical rough-in assist under supervision (conduit, disconnects, three-phase supply awareness). Introduction to mechanical drawings and equipment schedules (full competence with commercial drawings is a later NCCER Level 4 skill — 03401; months 1–2 is reading a simple sheet with a lead). EPA 608 Core + Type II; add Type III if installing or starting low-pressure systems.

**Days 61–90.** Assist start-up: leak test, evacuate, charge, electrical checks, and documentation with a lead. Gas piping and venting only if authorized. Coordination with other trades (controls, sheet metal, electrical). Quality and commissioning awareness: record as-built data, model/serial numbers, and start-up sheets (ACCA QI documentation principles still apply on light-commercial work). Ready-to-Work when ready.

**Months 3–6.** Repeatable light-commercial installation: RTU changeouts, split systems, duct and pipe connections, start-up documentation. Hydronic component recognition if the contractor does it (NCCER introduction to hydronic systems is commonly year two). Crew communication and layout from drawings. Sales-adjacent: explain options and warranties to a facility contact only if that is the contractor’s process.

**Months 6–12.** Progress toward second-chair or lead-helper on standard RTU or split installs after sign-off. System start-up and shutdown procedures (NCCER includes start-up/shutdown in later levels; treat months 6–12 as assisted competence). Zoning, ductless, or VRF only with manufacturer training (these systems are easy to damage with poor install practice). NATE Support Technician at 6–12 months; NATE Installation specialties around the one- to two-year mark per NATE guidance. Crew-leadership modules are later (NCCER fundamentals of crew leadership is a late module).

### 5. Sales track (for service techs and installers — not a standalone week-1 path)

This track is **additive**. ACCA’s Technician and Client Communication Training (TACCT) is designed for technicians already running service, maintenance, or installation visits. Putting a brand-new helper into maintenance-agreement closing in week 1 conflicts with both skill reality and trust.

**Week 1 (light, all technical paths).** First impressions, appearance, courtesy, and not overstating knowledge. Smile, treat customers with respect, and ask if the work met expectations (HVAC School). Do not present repair-vs-replace options yet.

**First 30–60 days.** Role clarity: the tech’s job is to inspect thoroughly, document, and explain what they *saw*. Practice plain-language descriptions (“the drain is clogged; water was in the pan”) without pitching. Learn the company’s maintenance visit outline or install turnover checklist (TACCT includes a baseline job outline and pre-flight checklists for service, maintenance, and installation).

**Days 61–90 through months 3–6.** Once the person can complete a consistent maintenance or install walkthrough: how to develop and explain **options**; how to be a client advocate; “leave the system better than you found it” (ACCA TACCT learning objectives). ACCA Skill Builder guidance from Matt Akins: present **three options** — (1) higher investment that restores operation plus preventive work plus an enhancement, (2) restore operation plus preventive work, (3) an “off-ramp” repair-only to get the system running — and tie warranties to solving the root cause rather than a bandage repair. Identify the root cause before presenting options.

**Months 6–12.** Convert appropriate clients to membership / maintenance-agreement customers (TACCT explicitly includes this). Navigate objections without pressure or misrepresentation. For commercial accounts, present recommendations with objectives, equipment details, benefits, terms, and justification (ACCA commercial service-sales guidance). Installers: good/better/best equipment or accessory options at proposal or job-turnover time, only after technical competence on what is being offered. This is technician consultative selling, not a full residential comfort-advisor or design-sales role (NATE/DOE have a separate “Heat Pump Comfort Advisor” recognition path; that is beyond this first-year sales track).

**Uncertainty:** Vendor sales courses (Lennox Tech Communications and various contractor sales trainers) exist but were not used as technical standards. Company scripts and pricing ethics vary; the app should teach process and communication, not high-pressure tactics.

## (c) Personalization rules

Use these rules in Match My Path to change **starting module**, **order**, and **certification prompts**. When in doubt, keep the safety module and a short placement quiz rather than skipping blindly.

### Inputs to collect

- **Experience level:** none; helper 0–6 months; 6–12 months; 1–2 years; 2+ years / journey-level.
- **Role / goal:** residential service, residential install, commercial service, commercial install; plus whether they want the sales add-on. Allow dual goals (e.g., residential service + sales).
- **Schooling:** none; some courses; completed trade-school or community-college HVAC program; registered apprentice (year if known).
- **Prior certifications:** EPA 608 (Core only / Type I / Type II / Type III / Universal); OSHA 10 or 30 (Construction vs. General Industry); first aid/CPR; NATE Ready-to-Work; NATE Support Technician; NATE Core + specialty (which ones); HVAC Excellence / ESCO Employment Ready exams; state license (type).
- **Equipment exposure** (seen, assisted, or worked independently): gas furnace, oil furnace, electric heat, air-source heat pump, dual fuel, ductless/mini-split, packaged RTU, hydronic/boiler, chilled water, centrifugal or other low-pressure chiller, supermarket/commercial refrigeration, VRF, BAS/controls.
- **Work context:** climate / heating- vs cooling-first season; residential vs commercial employer; whether the shop has a certified supervisor for refrigerant work.

### Placement rules

1. **Safety is never fully skipped**, but it can be a short employer-and-path delta (roof/fall, LOTO, A2L, combustion) if the learner already completed OSHA Outreach and can pass a safety check.
2. **If experience = none:** start at Week 1 shared foundation. Do not unlock unsupervised refrigerant, live electrical diagnosis, or sales-closing modules.
3. **If 0–6 months:** start after a placement quiz on safety, tools, cycle, and electrical safety. Skip only modules scored as mastered. Prioritize EPA 608 if they will open circuits; prioritize Ready-to-Work.
4. **If 6–12 months:** start at path-specific maintenance/install-quality and electrical-diagrams modules; recommend NATE Support Technician; unlock Sales track core (options, memberships) if the technical checklist for a consistent call/install is met.
5. **If 1–2 years:** start at troubleshooting / quality-install verification / commercial airside or hydronic delta as appropriate; prompt NATE Installation specialty (~1 year per FAQ) or continue building toward NATE Service (~2 years per FAQ).
6. **If 2+ years:** path-delta and advanced modules; certification gap-fill; mentoring/crew skills later.
7. **EPA 608 already held:** skip exam-prep; add a short practice-reminder and A2L/low-GWP safety if equipment exposure includes newer refrigerants or the learner has no A2L training. If they hold only Type I, prompt Type II before residential/light-commercial high-pressure work. If they will service low-pressure chillers and lack Type III or Universal, block those tasks in the path and prompt Type III.
8. **No EPA 608, and not a closely supervised apprentice:** do not assign recovery, charging, or gauge-and-hose tasks as independent practice. Study of the cycle and P-T charts is still appropriate.
9. **OSHA 10 Construction already held:** skip the Outreach course module; keep tool, electrical, refrigerant, and path-specific safety. If they hold **General Industry** 10-hour only, note that construction jobsites and many HVAC employers expect **Construction** Outreach; this is a common requirement pattern, not a federal OSHA mandate.
10. **Trade-school complete:** offer a comprehensive placement test mapped to ESCO/NCCER Level 1 outcomes (cycle, electricity, piping, 608, install/maintenance). Start in the first failed domain; send passing domains to “review library” not the main queue. Still require supervised field hour goals — school is not a substitute for OJT.
11. **Registered apprentice:** align module order to their program (NCCER vs. UA). UA paths should prompt OSHA-30 if that is the local standard; ABC/NCCER paths often prompt OSHA 10 in year one.
12. **Goal = installer:** move piping, brazing, drawings, setting equipment, ACCA QI documentation, and start-up earlier; keep diagnostics lighter until after quality-install skills.
13. **Goal = service:** move maintenance, measurements (superheat, subcool, delta T, amps), schematics, and troubleshooting earlier; still teach enough brazing and recovery to be useful.
14. **Goal includes sales:** attach Sales track after, not instead of, technical modules. Unlock option-presenting after “consistent maintenance or install walkthrough” competency. Never unlock memberships/agreements in week 1 for a complete beginner.
15. **Equipment exposure — heating-first:** after shared cycle and electrical safety, insert gas or oil heat modules before deep cooling diagnostics (and the reverse in cooling-first climates).
16. **Equipment exposure — heat pumps or ductless listed as “independent”:** skip intro recognition; start at defrost, charge in heat mode, or mini-split commissioning/troubleshooting as appropriate.
17. **Equipment exposure — RTUs:** for residential-goal learners who actually work on packaged commercial equipment, add commercial PM and roof safety even if their “goal” is residential.
18. **Equipment exposure — chillers or low-pressure systems:** add Type III, mechanical-room / ASHRAE 15 awareness (EPA Type III topics reference ASHRAE Standard 15 equipment-room requirements), and a delayed start on independent chiller work.
19. **Equipment exposure — none:** keep examples generic (residential split + furnace + basic RTU photos) until a path is chosen.
20. **Conflicting goals** (e.g., “commercial chillers” + “0 experience”): follow beginner foundation first; put chiller modules in months 6–12+ as a goal path, with a clear explanation that this is not a week-1 task.
21. **Competency gates, not dates:** a learner may remain in “first 30 days” modules until they pass safety and tool checks, regardless of calendar time. Conversely, a trade-school graduate may reach days-61–90 technical modules in the first month of OJT if checks pass.

## (e) Practical on-the-job first tasks per path

All first tasks assume **supervision**, lockout/tagout where energy is present, and no independent refrigerant-circuit work until EPA 608 is held (unless the person is an apprentice **closely and continually supervised** under 40 CFR 82.161).

### Residential Service Tech

**Earliest tasks:** ride-along; carry and organize tools; change and report air filters; inspect and clear condensate drains and pans; help wash condenser coils; visual inspection (oil stains, rub-outs on copper, damaged control wiring, dirty blower wheels or evap coils using a mirror/flashlight, missing screws/caps); confirm disconnects and thermostat setpoints on the way out; greet the customer and explain what was checked; complete the work order.

**After fundamentals and (as applicable) 608:** assist with recovery and charging; measure delta T; observe superheat/subcooling; replace a capacitor or contactor under a lead tech after verifying the diagnosis; perform a complete maintenance with a run test **at the end** (HVAC School: do not leave disconnects out).

**Defer:** unsupervised no-cool diagnosis, compressor replacement decisions, gas-manifold or combustion analysis without training, any “replace the system” recommendation without a documented root cause.

### Residential Installer

**Earliest tasks:** protect the home; stage materials; team-lift and set equipment on a prepared pad or attic platform under a lead; run and support flex duct; help hang or connect line sets (mechanical work before unsupervised brazing); insulate suction lines; run condensate; seal penetrations; pull thermostat cable; cleanup and haul-away assist; collect model/serial numbers.

**After piping/brazing training and 608:** braze under supervision with nitrogen purge practice as trained; pressure test; evacuate to a deep vacuum and hold; assist start-up and charge; set a thermostat; walk the customer through filter access and basic operation; complete start-up documentation.

**Defer:** solo equipment setting in unsafe attic or roof conditions; unsupervised electrical lugs on new circuits; gas piping without authorization; design (Manual J/D/S) without training; final charge sign-off without airflow verification.

### Commercial Service Tech

**Earliest tasks:** site check-in; roof and mechanical-room safety; LOTO with a lead; replace RTU or air-handler filters; inspect belts and report wear/tension (do not adjust until shown); coil cleaning assist; record nameplate data and log readings the lead requests; housekeeping on the roof; never leave panels unsecured.

**After fundamentals and 608:** belt replacement and pulley alignment under supervision; supervised electrical measurements (amps, voltage) on packaged units; PM plus basic repairs (contactors, capacitors, drain issues); read BAS alarms/trends with a controls or senior tech; document findings for a facility manager.

**Defer:** independent chiller work; BAS programming; unsupervised three-phase motor replacement; entering operating equipment rooms without required refrigerant/oxygen sensors awareness and site rules (ASHRAE 15 appears in EPA Type III topics); any confined-space entry without training and a permit process.

### Commercial Installer

**Earliest tasks:** site orientation and PPE; stay clear of crane/rigging unless assigned and trained; stage curb caps, duct transitions, and accessories; assist setting and aligning a unit with a lead; hang or connect duct; assist pipe supports; insulate; clean the work area; as-built photo and model/serial documentation.

**After piping/electrical assist skills and 608:** make up piping and brazed joints under supervision; assist electrical connections; leak test, evacuate, and start-up assist; complete commissioning sheets with a lead; coordinate punch-list items.

**Defer:** signaling a crane without qualification; unsupervised gas or high-voltage terminations; fire-stopping unless trained and allowed on that site; VRF or chiller start-up without manufacturer training and the right 608 type.

### Sales (on service and install calls)

**Earliest tasks:** professional introduction; do not guess; narrate simple findings; ask whether anything else is needed; leave the site clean.

**After a consistent maintenance or install process:** present findings, root cause, and three options (restore + preventive + enhancement; restore + preventive; repair-only off-ramp); explain maintenance agreements / memberships as protection and planned care, not a pressure close; document the customer’s choice.

**Defer:** pricing equipment replacements without the company’s estimating process; disparaging prior contractors; selling repairs that were not verified.

## How this maps to the app

- **Match My Path** should ask the inputs in section (c), then output a recommended sequence (foundation → path modules → certifications → sales add-on if selected) and the next 3–5 concrete steps (e.g., “This week: safety + tools; schedule OSHA 10; study refrigeration cycle; ride along and only do filters/drains”).
- **Classes/modules** should be tagged by `path`, `stage`, and `prerequisites`, and filtered to the learner’s path while still showing shared fundamentals.
- **Internal tests** can follow Employment Ready / Ready-to-Work style domains early, then path finals later, always distinct from EPA/NATE.
- Formal apprenticeships remain the gold-standard long path (multi-year, thousands of OJT hours). The app should say so.
